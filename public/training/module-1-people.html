    What organizing means

    “Organizing is when people combine the individual resources they have into the shared power that they need to achieve a common purpose.”

    Organizing is a leadership practice: taking responsibility for helping others reach a shared goal when the outcome is uncertain. Organizers find, recruit and develop leaders, build a group of supporters around them, and turn what those supporters have (time, relationships, skills, votes) into the power to win. It works because it turns voluntary effort based on real commitment into the capacity to make change.

    This program has three modules, one for each part of that definition:

    Module 1

    People

    How can I stand more fully in my own power, help others stand in theirs, and understand how decisions get made?

    Module 3

    Purpose

    How do we plan and run campaigns that win, build leaders and set us up for long-term progress?

    Issues, interests & values: understanding self-interest

    Key takeaways

    • Self-interest isn’t selfish. It means “self with others,” and it’s the basis of trust.
    • Issue = the public problem · Interest = your personal stake · Value = the principle underneath.
    • Being open about why you are here helps voters and teammates trust you.
    📖 Prefer to read? Open the full lesson

    Self-interest isn’t selfishness. It’s the honest answer to “why am I here?”, and it’s where trust starts.

    Selfish

    Puts self before others.

    Selfless

    Puts others before self. It’s often praised, but it’s hard to sustain and can hide our real motives.

    Self-interest

    Understands self with others. It’s the basis for relationships and acting together.

    Our culture often motivates people through coercion (“do this or you’re fired”), and activist spaces too often rely on shame or guilt. Lasting action comes from caring about others and about ourselves, and from knowing what drives us: the issues we care about, our personal interests in those issues, and the values underneath.

    Being clear about your self-interest also builds trust. If people can’t tell why you’re involved, they’ll wonder about your motives.

    “If you have come here to help me, you are wasting your time. But if you have come because your liberation is bound up with mine, then let us work together.”— Lilla Watson

    Section 3· 15 min

    Public narrative: Story of Self, Us and Now

    Key takeaways

    • Stories move people to act because they carry values through emotion.
    • Every story has three parts: Challenge → Choice → Outcome.
    • Self: why I’m called · Us: why we’re called · Now: why we must act today, ending with a clear, hopeful choice.
    • Your values tie the three stories together. Practice, and adapt your story to each audience.
    📖 Prefer to read? Open the full lesson

    Facts inform people, but stories move them to act. Public narrative, a framework developed by Marshall Ganz of Harvard University, teaches you to turn your values into action through stories.

    Head

    Strategy

    How we can act to change things.

    Heart

    Motivation

    Why we should act. Narrative lives here.

    Hands

    Action

    People acting together with purpose.

    Values inspire action through emotion. Emotions tell us what we value in ourselves, in others and in the world. Stories let us share values as lived experience rather than abstract principles, which is why they can move people. We each have stories of challenge (or we wouldn’t think the world needs changing) and stories of hope (or we wouldn’t think we could change it).

    The structure of every story: Challenge → Choice → Outcome

    Story of Self: why I am called

    Tell a story of self to share the values that called you to act. Without it, people will guess at your motives, often wrongly. Focus on choice points: moments when your values shaped a decision. When did you first care about being heard? When did you first see injustice? When did you feel you had to act, and why did you believe you could? Include sensory detail (the place, the sounds, what it looked like). Share the moments that shaped your public life, not your deepest private secrets.

    Questions to dig deeper

    Challenge: Why was it a challenge? Why was it yours?

    Choice: Why did you choose what you did? Where did the courage or hope come from? Did family stories teach you how to act?

    Outcome: How did it feel? What did it teach you? What do you want to teach us?

    Where stories hide

    Family & childhood: parents, growing up, community, role models, school

    Life choices: school, career, family, passions, overcoming challenges

    Organizing experience: first time organizing, first election win, key people

    A challenge doesn’t have to be a misfortune. It can be a mountain you chose to climb just as much as a valley you climbed out of. And you are the main character, not a friend or relative.

    Example: how Dimple’s public story fits the structure

    Challenge: Arriving in America at 16 with a language barrier, then working through college, including cleaning hotel rooms. Later, the sudden loss of her father at 55.
    Choice: She kept going, became a CPA, and after her father’s death left a successful finance career for public service.
    Outcome: Today she uses that budget experience on City Council. The values it shows are hard work, resilience and service.

    You can use this outline when voters ask “Who is she?” Then tell your own story of why you support her.

    Story of Us: why we are called

    In a story of us, the character is the group you’re asking to act. Shared stories express the identity and values of communities: family, faith, neighborhood, and new ones like this campaign. Focus on a specific story about specific people at a specific time that reminds everyone of the values you share. It takes the courage of empathy: imagining others’ experience well enough to put it into words.

    • Challenge: what your community has faced or faces now. Make it real with images and details, not statistics.
    • Choice: moments when the community met a challenge with action. These are a source of hope.
    • Outcome (hope): vivid reminders of what you’ve achieved together.

    The goal is to help people see themselves as a community and believe they can make a difference.

    Story of Now: why we must act now

    A story of now names an urgent challenge the “us” faces, paints a nightmare (what happens if we don’t act) and a dream (what’s possible if we do), and ends with a specific, hopeful choice each listener can make right now. It connects story (why we should act) with strategy (how we can act). It takes the courage of imagination: naming the pain and the possibility.

    Leaders who only describe problems aren’t much help. If you’ve made people feel the urgency, you owe them a way to act that could work. A story of now is a call to hopeful action.

    Story of now on the doors: “Our next city election will decide who shapes Charlotte’s budget, housing and public safety. If people like us stay home, those decisions get made without us. Dimple has a record of delivering. Can we count on your vote, and will you tell three neighbors?”

    Putting it together

    A linked public narrative connects why you are called, why we are called, and what our mission asks of us now. Your values are what link the three. Storytelling isn’t linear: working on your story of now may change your story of self. The goal isn’t a fixed script. It’s a process you can adapt to any audience or moment, and it gets better with practice.

    Coaching someone else’s story: what to listen for

    Balance positive and constructive feedback. Skip vague praise (“That was great!”) and coach on specifics:

    • Challenge: “When you described ___, I got a clear picture of the challenge.” / “The challenge wasn’t clear. How would you describe ___?”
    • Choice: “To me, the choice you made was ___, and it made me feel ___.” / “Try focusing on the moment you made the choice.”
    • Outcome: “I understood the outcome to be ___, and it teaches me ___. How does it relate to your work now?”
    • Values: “It’s clear you value ___. It could be even clearer if you told us where that value comes from.”
    • Details: “The image of ___ really helped me feel what you felt.” / “Tell us more about ___ so we can picture it.”

    Section 4· 12 min

    1:1 organizing conversations

    Key takeaways

    • Frame it → get their story (listen 70%+) → assess alignment → make the ask.
    • Make asks SMART and yes-or-no: “Will you canvass Saturday at 10 a.m.?”, not “Come by sometime?”
    • Empathize, then educate. Ask questions more than you lecture.
    • Follow up when you said you would.
    📖 Prefer to read? Open the full lesson

    A 1:1 is a structured conversation that builds a public relationship as the basis for acting together on shared issues, interests and values. Good 1:1s are how we recruit volunteers, win over voters and develop leaders.

    The 70% rule

    A good 1:1 is at least 70% listening. Share enough of your own story to show your stake and to model honesty, but focus on theirs.

    Start by framing the conversation: who you are, what you’re doing and why you want to talk. Then:

    1. Get their story

      Ask questions, and really listen, to learn what they care about. Start broad (“If you could change three things about your neighborhood tomorrow, what would they be?”), then ask follow-ups that get at their personal stake and underlying values. Go from the surface issue to “why does that matter to you?” to the value underneath.

    2. Assess alignment

      Based on what you heard, look at three things:

      Alignment

      Do they share our approach and goals?

      Influence

      Do they have time, skills or relationships to contribute?

      Commitment

      How likely are they to follow through? How deep is their self-interest?

    3. Set a commitment (make the ask)

      If there’s alignment, ask for a next step: a follow-up meeting, a volunteer shift, a pledge to vote, an event. Make it SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) and ask it as a direct yes-or-no question.

    Spot the stronger ask

    Weak: “Will you come to one of our future events?”
    Strong: “Will you join our canvass this Saturday at 10 a.m., starting from the east side staging location, to talk with voters about Dimple?”

    🧩 Try it

    Then help them plan: How will you get there? Do you need childcare? Can I text you a reminder Friday? And follow up when you said you would. The ask has to connect to something they care about, or the commitment won’t hold.

    Going deeper: educate, inoculate, agitate

    Some people already understand an issue. Many hold mixed or contradictory ideas. The rule is empathize, then educate. People don’t change their minds when they feel unheard, judged or misunderstood. Listen for the feelings, values and unmet needs under their opinions, find what you share, and ask questions more than you make statements.

    When someone blames individuals for a shared problem

    “Do you know anyone who tried really hard and still couldn’t solve this? Why not?” “What support do you wish you’d had?”

    When someone blames another group

    “Do you know anyone in that group? Do you think they face this too?” “Might you have interests in common?” “Who benefits when we’re divided?”

    When someone believes a false solution

    “Have you tried that? What happened?” “I’ve seen people try ___. What would happen if we all did that?”

    Four techniques that make persuasion stick

    Self-persuasion

    tap to flip
    People change their minds more readily than they accept being told what to think.

    Active processing

    tap to flip
    Questions that make people think hard produce bigger, longer-lasting changes in attitude.

    Perspective-taking

    tap to flip
    Ask people to recall experiences that help them understand how others feel. This reduces prejudice.

    Cognitive dissonance

    tap to flip
    When someone’s lived experience contradicts an opinion they stated, they may revise the opinion.

    In medicine, inoculation builds immunity. In organizing, it means preparing supporters in advance for misleading claims, fear tactics and attempts to divide them, so they aren’t caught off guard. Use questions to help people:

    1. Understand why an opponent might oppose our efforts
    2. Anticipate the specific arguments and tactics they’ll use
    3. Prepare how we’ll respond

    Before Election Day, talk supporters through what they might hear: rumors about voting times or places, confusion about ID rules, or false claims about the candidate. Point them to official sources (the Mecklenburg County Board of Elections) and campaign staff. Never repeat or spread unverified claims yourself.

    Solidarity is the antidote: opponents win when they divide us, and we win when we come together.

    Agitation is a caring challenge that helps someone see the attitudes, beliefs or behaviors holding them back from their full power. It only works within a trusting relationship and must be rooted in their self-interest, so it usually comes in a later conversation, not a first 1:1. Module 2 covers agitation in depth.

    Section 5· 10 min

    Listening and compassionate presence

    Key takeaways

    • Listen for the need behind a feeling (respect, safety, being heard) instead of arguing.
    • Center yourself before a conversation: stand tall, balance, breathe, relax your jaw and shoulders.
    • Notice your energy after a tough door, and reset before the next one.
    📖 Prefer to read? Open the full lesson

    You can’t build real relationships while distracted, defensive or rushed. These practices help you stay grounded and truly hear the voter in front of you, even at the fortieth door of the day.

    Nonviolent Communication (NVC): feelings and needs

    Psychologist Marshall Rosenberg developed NVC to help people say what matters to them and hear what matters to others. It isn’t an organizing method itself, but it builds the empathy, listening and emotional intelligence that organizing needs.

    Needs are universal

    Needs are what’s most alive in us: our core values and deepest longings. Everyone needs rest, shelter, food, connection and more, even if we feel them to different degrees.

    Feelings signal needs

    Met needs bring joy, gratitude, hope. Unmet needs bring sadness, anger, frustration. Feelings are neither right nor wrong.

    Our strategies for meeting needs can conflict, but the needs themselves are shared. If you can boil a disagreement down to feelings and needs, people start to see each other as human beings and solutions get easier to find.

    A voter snaps, “You people only show up at election time.” Instead of arguing, listen for the need: to be respected and heard. Try: “It sounds like you’re frustrated that nobody checks in between elections. That matters. What would you want your council member to know?”

    The four dimensions of centering
    • Length: line up head, shoulders, hips, knees and ankles so you’re not fighting gravity. Feel the dignity of standing tall.
    • Width: spread your weight evenly between both feet, hips and shoulders. Feel your connection to others and the world.
    • Depth: don’t lean forward or back on your heels. Feel the support behind you: your history and the people who came before you.
    • Center: drop your attention to your belly, your center of gravity.

    At each step, check whether you’re relaxed but alert. Are your eyes, jaw and shoulders relaxed? Is your breath deep and low in your belly? Under pressure, notice where you tighten (jaw, chest, stomach), then let go and come back to center. The practice isn’t about stiff posture. It’s about feeling your aliveness.

    Noticing sensations and emotions

    The fastest way to become present is to notice your physical sensations: temperature (cool, warm, sweating), pressure (tightness, contraction, relaxation) and movement (pulse, breath, tingling). Emotions are short-lived. Moods last longer and can open or close relationships. Emotions you don’t deal with can settle into moods (unresolved anger can become resentment).

    Section 6· 10 min

    Base building: constituency, community, base

    Key takeaways

    • Constituency → community (sees itself as “us”) → base (takes action).
    • Leaders are people others follow. That’s not the same as having a title. “Organizer” is a role anyone can play.
    • Organize supporters, win over the middle, and don’t get stuck on firm opposition.
    • Help people climb the leadership ladder one rung at a time, and invite them personally.
    📖 Prefer to read? Open the full lesson

    Campaigns win by turning a constituency into a community and a community into an active base. These ideas are adapted from the work of Hashim Benford.

    Constituency

    The people an organization serves and organizes, often defined by geography, demographics, economics or institutions. For this campaign, that’s Charlotte voters citywide.

    Community

    A constituency that sees itself as an “us” with shared interests. Sharing traits doesn’t automatically create that feeling. Organizers help people discover what they have in common.

    Base

    People who feel part of the community and take action: vote, volunteer, donate, attend, share.

    Example from the training: two neighborhoods, one campaign

    A mostly Black Southside and a largely Latino Eastside both have far fewer parks than the wealthier Northside, but the two neighborhoods don’t see themselves as an “us.” An organizer brings young people from both together around their frustration. They petition, hold events, turn out to the city budget hearings and rally for more courts and bathrooms in both areas. Everyone who takes part becomes the base, and through the campaign the neighborhoods start to see themselves as one community.

    Mapping who we talk to

    We map people on two axes, always relative to a specific issue, community and ask:

    Alignment

    How closely do they share our view?
    Strong supporter · Undecided / mixed (most people!) · Firmly opposed

    Influence

    How much do they shape what others think and do?
    High · Medium · Low

    Organize supporters, win over the middle, don’t get stuck on firm opposition

    Organize our supporters: connect strong supporters and develop them as leaders.
    Win over the middle: as supporters take action together, they persuade undecided people by showing why joining is in their interest.
    Don’t get stuck on firm opposition: some people won’t move. Be courteous, thank them, and spend your limited time where it counts. As more people join us, opposition has less influence.

    The source packet calls these groups “advanced, intermediate and backwards” and the principle “unite the advanced, win over the middle, isolate the backwards.” We use campaign-friendly language here.

    The leadership ladder

    Think of each supporter’s journey as a ladder. They start at the bottom, interested but not committed. Each rung is a deeper commitment, and the safest way up is one rung at a time.

    Story: Ruby Duncan and the Welfare Mamas

    Ruby Duncan started working in Louisiana fields at age 8 and later moved to Nevada for better opportunities. After an on-the-job injury, she relied on welfare to support her children. When her friend Olestina Walker invited her to a welfare mothers’ organizing meeting, Ruby said, “You gotta be crazy! We can’t go down there asking people for money.” Olestina came to her house anyway: “Ruby, you are going with me.”

    At that first meeting Ruby learned words like “legislature” and who made the decisions affecting her life. She marched with a small group around the legislative building. When they got a meeting with lawmakers, the others encouraged her to speak. She never thought she’d be the one, but telling them how the Welfare Department treated her came easily. When the legislators said there was no money, she got angrier and more vocal. She joined the National Welfare Rights Organization and helped plan the biggest welfare rights march in the history of the Las Vegas Strip.

    The lesson: Ruby climbed one rung at a time: show up, march, speak, lead. And it started because someone personally asked her, twice.

    Key roles: flip to learn

    Activists

    tap to flip
    The most active people in the base. They regularly canvass, phone bank, attend trainings and events.

    Leaders

    tap to flip
    People others look to for advice and direction. This isn’t the same as formal authority: the neighbor everyone calls when there’s a problem is a leader.

    Organizers

    tap to flip
    Coordinate the base and develop other leaders. Organizer is a role, not a job title. Volunteers can be organizers. A campaign’s scale depends on how many it has.

    Strategic leaders

    tap to flip
    Responsible for strategic decisions, and for developing the skills and analysis of other organizers.

    Members

    tap to flip
    Part of the base who’ve made a defined commitment (for example signing on or pledging a level of activity). Every member is in the base, but not everyone in the base is a member.

    Authority

    tap to flip
    A formal position, like an HOA president. People may or may not follow them. Don’t confuse authority with leadership.

    Section 7· 6 min

    Understanding barriers and equity

    Key takeaways

    • Barriers can build up at four levels: individual, interpersonal, institutional, structural.
    • Neighborhoods have different histories. Your safety and opportunity shouldn’t depend on your zip code.
    • Go into every neighborhood curious, not assuming.
    📖 Prefer to read? Open the full lesson

    People don’t all start from the same place. To organize across Charlotte, it helps to understand how disadvantages build up across different levels of society.

    The source training describes systems of oppression: organized decisions, structures and ideas that consistently disadvantage some groups and advantage others. They show up at four levels:

    Micro

    Individual

    Bias and prejudice inside a person, including internalized beliefs about one’s own group.

    Interpersonal

    Bias acted out between people.

    Institutional

    Unequal treatment built into an organization’s policies and practices.

    Macro

    Structural

    Patterns across many institutions and society as a whole.

    The packet looks at how race, class and gender disadvantages work at each level, and argues they are deeply intertwined rather than separate or ranked. It discusses intersectionality: for example, a Black woman’s experience differs from both a white woman’s and a Black man’s. It warns that ranking one form of inequality above another tends to pit communities against each other.

    Why this matters at the door

    Dimple’s first priority is a safe Charlotte whatever your zip code. Neighborhoods have different histories of investment in sidewalks, parks, housing and jobs. When you listen for the barriers a voter has faced, and don’t assume everyone’s experience matches yours, you build trust faster and help people see how their concerns connect to their neighbors’.

    Practice: Before your next shift, look up the neighborhood you’ll canvass. What’s changed there recently? What might residents care about most? Go in curious, not assuming.
