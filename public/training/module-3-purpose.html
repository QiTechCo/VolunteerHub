Purpose: Turning Commitment Into Wins

How do we use our power to accomplish real change? This module covers personal goal setting and time management, campaign planning from issue to tactics, long-term strategy, and the art of giving and receiving feedback.

    Section 1· 5 min

    Purpose and the anatomy of a plan

    Key takeaways

    • Purpose is your “why,” and asking “why?” again and again gets you to it.
    • Every plan has four parts: goal, analysis, strategy, tactics.
    📖 Prefer to read? Open the full lesson

    A plan is a sequence of actions aimed at a purpose. Most plans, whether or not we notice it, have four parts:

    Goal

    Where do we want to go?

    Analysis

    Where are we now?

    Strategy

    What overall approach gets us there?

    Tactics

    How will we carry it out?

    We’ll use this framework twice in this module: first for your own life and time, then for a campaign.

    🧩 Try it

    Purpose is the intention that moves us to act: our “why,” our North Star. It gets us out of bed, keeps us going when things are hard, and gets us back on track when we drift.

    Purposes are often nested. Keep asking “why?” and the answers get deeper:

    …to help Dimple win the at-large seat.
    …so the Council keeps a leader who has fought for affordable housing and safer neighborhoods.
    …so families like mine can afford to stay in Charlotte and feel safe here.
    …because I believe everyone deserves a stable, safe home and a fair shot. That’s the purpose.
    Section 2· 12 min

    Your purpose, milestones and workload

    Key takeaways

    • Work backward: life purpose → 3-year milestone → 3-month stepping stone.
    • Put your big rocks on the calendar first, then fit in the pebbles and sand.
    • Don’t let “urgent” crowd out “important.” Do, schedule, delegate or drop.
    • Build in 15–30 minutes of self-care every day.
    📖 Prefer to read? Open the full lesson

    Big rocks, pebbles and sand

    Stephen Covey’s demonstration: fill a jar with big rocks first, then pebbles and sand fill the gaps. Start with sand and pebbles, and the big rocks won’t fit. If our days fill up with little emergencies and errands, we never get to what matters most. Pebbles and sand (chores, obligations, busywork) are part of life. They just shouldn’t go in first.

    How to plan a week with more big rocks
    1. Pick 4–7 big rock activities for the week.
    2. Add what you’ve already committed to on your calendar.
    3. Schedule each big rock at a time you won’t be rushed or tempted to cancel.
    4. Build in 15–30 minutes of self-care every day. The training calls this a requirement.
    5. Keep a “laundry list” of smaller tasks, grouped by type or length. Some will roll to next week, and that’s fine.
    6. The night before or first thing in the morning, choose which list items fit. Don’t fill every slot, so you have room for surprises.
    7. At the end of each day, celebrate what you did, big and small.

    Urgent vs. important: the Eisenhower Matrix

    Research on the “mere-urgency effect” (Journal of Consumer Research) found that we pick time-sensitive tasks over important ones even when the important task pays off more. When people stop to think about consequences, they choose better. Sort these volunteer tasks:

    Urgent + Important → Do

    • Do immediately

    Not urgent + Important → Schedule

    • Put it on the calendar

    Urgent + Less important → Delegate

    • Hand it to someone else

    Not urgent + Less important → Delete

    • Let it go

    Eisenhower Matrix material excerpted in the packet from Laura Scroggs, todoist.com.

    Section 3· 6 min

    What makes a campaign

    Key takeaways

    • A campaign is a series of planned moves over time toward a specific goal.
    • Set three objectives: external (win), internal (build leaders), narrative (shift the story).
    • For us: win the seat, build a lasting volunteer base, and show a Charlotte that’s safe and affordable everywhere.
    📖 Prefer to read? Open the full lesson

    A campaign is a series of planned moves, carried out over time, to reach a specific goal. Powerful campaigns are proactive: they don’t just react, they take the initiative and shape the agenda.

    Goal

    Where do we want to go?

    • External objective: what outcome will we win? (target + demand)
    • Internal objective: what skills and leadership will we build?
    • Narrative objective: what ideas, beliefs and feelings will we change?
    Analysis

    Where are we now?

    • Resources: what do we have?
    • Opposition: what stands in our way?
    • Support: who or what can help us?
    Strategy

    How do we get there?

    • Target: who has the power to give us what we want?
    • Strategy: how will we get them to do it?
    • Rationale: why do we think it will work?
    Tactics

    How will we carry it out?

    The concrete activities that carry out the strategy.

    Planning is iterative: refining one part often means revisiting another. Try several templates and find what works, but generally hold off picking tactics until you’ve analyzed the situation, cut your issue and chosen your target.

    The three objectives on an electoral campaign

    External: win the at-large seat. In an election, the “target” is the voters, and the demand is their vote.
    Internal: build a volunteer base, from first-time canvassers to team captains, that outlasts Election Day.
    Narrative: help Charlotte see a city that’s safe and affordable in every neighborhood as possible, and Dimple as someone who delivers it.
    Section 4· 10 min

    Cutting the issue and setting goals

    Key takeaways

    • Cut the issue: turn a big problem into a specific, winnable fight (like “dust,” not “the mine”).
    • Every demand needs a target who can say yes and a timeline.
    • Right-size it: a stretch, but within reach.
    📖 Prefer to read? Open the full lesson

    You can’t win “fix housing.” You can win a specific decision by a specific decision-maker by a specific date.

    Cutting an issue means breaking a large problem into a focused issue you can win. People need that focus to act, and each win builds a record that attracts more people. There are usually many ways to cut the same problem.

    Example from the training: the uranium mine

    Environmental campaigners in Alice Springs, Australia, opposed a proposed uranium mine. They could have cut the issue many ways: water, jobs, Indigenous land rights, transport, health. They chose dust. It was immediate, specific and winnable. Everyone in town already worried about dust storms, and the image of children breathing dust from a uranium mine brought the community together.

    Set the external objective: demand, target, timeline

    Transformative

    Get a decision-maker to solve the issue.

    Combative

    Remove the source of the issue.

    Alternative

    Use your own resources to build another way to address it.

    • Target: the person or body that can actually say yes. Make sure they have that power, which may take research.
    • Timeline: how long you expect it will take to win.
    • Right-sized: ambitious enough to stretch you, but not out of reach. If it’ll take ten years, it’s a long-term mission. Break it into smaller campaigns.

    Section 5· 10 min

    Analysis, strategy and tactics

    Key takeaways

    • Analyze first, both external and internal, before choosing tactics.
    • “Tactics without strategy is the noise before defeat.”
    • Our tactics: canvassing, phone/text banking, signs, house meetings, poll greeting, relational organizing.
    📖 Prefer to read? Open the full lesson

    Analysis: where are we right now?

    Many campaigners rush into action to look busy. Taking time to understand the situation is just as important.

    External (landscape) analysis

    What political, economic and cultural factors create or keep this problem going? What are the root causes? Who benefits from the status quo, and who would benefit from change? Are some groups hit harder? What are other groups already doing?

    Internal (organizational) analysis

    What are our strengths and limits? How high a priority is this? What resources can we count on? No organization can do everything. Use your particular strengths.

    Strategy: how do we get from here to there?

    “Strategy without tactics is the slowest route to victory. Tactics without strategy is the noise before defeat.”— Sun Tzu, The Art of War
    “Planning without action is futile. Action without planning is fatal.”— New Tactics in Human Rights

    Answer three questions: Target (who has the power?), Strategy (how will we get them to act?), Rationale (why will it work?).

    Tactics: the steps on the staircase

    Organizer Lee Staples (Roots to Power, 1984) compared strategy to a staircase and tactics to its steps. Choose tactics based on your analysis, strategy and objectives, not on habit or favorites. The training lists issue-campaign tactics in rising order of intensity: letter writing → petitions → public testimony → marching → picketing → blocking access → sit-ins. The packet also suggests scoring possible tactics against agreed criteria (it uses a “tactic star” model).

    Electoral tactics you’ll use on this campaign

    For a voter-contact campaign, the “staircase” moves voters from awareness to action. Each tactic serves the strategy of identifying supporters, persuading undecided voters and turning out our voters:

    🚪 Canvassing

    Face-to-face conversations: the most persuasive contact there is.

    📞 Phone & text banking

    Reach more voters quickly: ID, persuasion, reminders.

    🪧 Sign posting & lit drops

    Visibility and name recognition in every neighborhood.

    🏡 Event hosting

    House meetings and meet-and-greets that recruit new supporters and volunteers.

    🗳️ Poll greeting

    A last friendly reminder on the way in to vote.

    🤝 Relational organizing

    Reaching your own network, the people who already trust you.

    Section 6· 8 min

    The long-term agenda: making wins add up

    Key takeaways

    • A long-term agenda links today’s fights to a bigger vision.
    • Stepping stones → milestones → vision.
    • Dimple’s priorities are the pathways, her past wins are stepping stones, and this election is the next one.
    📖 Prefer to read? Open the full lesson

    How do individual wins add up to bigger change? A Long-Term Agenda (LTA), a Grassroots Power Project tool, maps how today’s fights connect to tomorrow’s goals.

    An LTA names bold, shared goals, then works backward to find the path and forward along it. It isn’t linear or ever finished. It’s a way to test a theory of change and adjust as conditions change. It works best when many groups use it to align their work.

    Where we want to be, 10 years out, or even 40. This vision orients all the work.

    Guideposts along the way, achievable within the timeframe. A reform is “structural” if it can shift power and resources toward communities and working people. Brainstorm them all, then sort them into stepping stones, milestones and structural reforms.

    The major fronts we move along. They overlap, and the best reforms cut across several. Naming them helps each group see where its issue fits in the bigger picture.

    How today’s campaigns lead to bigger wins. Not every campaign has to be structural, but each can open space for the next. Milestone reforms (10–15 years out) help us see “over the next ridge.”

    Two threads run through the whole process: strategic practice (changing how we work so every campaign becomes a stepping stone, like “different work, same playbook”) and narrative change (shifting the story to open space for bigger reforms).

    The packet also covers conjunctural analysis (“naming the moment”): studying how the social, political, economic and ideological forces of a period interact, because crises are moments when big change is possible but the outcome isn’t guaranteed. It notes that organized opponents have long pursued their own long-term agendas, which is a reason to plan with discipline over decades.

    Section 7· 10 min

    Giving and receiving assessments

    Key takeaways

    • Five speech acts: declaration, request, promise, assessment, assertion.
    • Ground every assessment in specific, observable examples.
    • When you receive one, remember: it’s their interpretation, not a fact about you.
    📖 Prefer to read? Open the full lesson

    Assessments are everywhere in our conversations. Calling someone “reliable” or a plan “risky” opens some possibilities and closes others. Many of us react to assessments automatically, with shame, fear or defensiveness, or treat one assessment as a permanent fact about who we are. These checklists help:

    Before delivering an assessment

    • Have I been given the authority to make it?
    • Am I competent to judge in this area?
    • For the sake of what? What future action or relationship do I want?
    • Am I in the right mood to give it? Are they in the right mood to hear it?
    • Should I set up how it’s heard first?
    • Can I ground it with specific, observable examples?

    When receiving an assessment

    • Did I give them the authority? Are they competent to judge?
    • Remember: it’s their interpretation, not true or false.
    • Am I in a good mood to receive it? Should I ask what it’s for?
    • Is it grounded?
    • What does it open up for me? Do I accept it?
    • Am I open to learning more from this person?
    • If my default reaction kicks in, return to center.

    Self-assessments

    Most of the assessments we make about ourselves are unasked for, negative and ungrounded. They’re old, learned chatter that shapes our moods and relationships without our noticing. Noticing them through reflection or a sitting practice weakens their hold. When we see that assessments can be changed, we can respect ourselves and others more.

    🧩 Try it

    When what we say matches what we do, people see us as trustworthy. Feedback is one of the most powerful and most mishandled forms of speech.

    This section is adapted from Richard Strozzi-Heckler’s Assessments for Action. Conversations for action rely on five speech acts that create commitment. Flip each one:

    Declaration

    tap to flip
    A commitment to a future possibility.
    “We will knock 10,000 doors this fall.”

    Request

    tap to flip
    A commitment to a future action.
    “Can you send me your turf numbers by Sunday at 8 p.m.?”

    Promise

    tap to flip
    A commitment to deliver.
    “I’ll have them to you by 8.”

    Assessment

    tap to flip
    A judgment that builds connection and shapes future action.
    “You did a great job with that tough voter.”

    Assertion

    tap to flip
    A statement about a reality that already exists.
    “We knocked 412 doors today.”

    Name the speech act:

    “Could you host a house party before October 15?”It asks for a future action.
    “Early voting starts on the date listed by the Board of Elections.”It states a fact that can be checked.
    “You’re a natural at phone banking.”It’s an opinion, an interpretation rather than a fact.
    “Yes. I’ll bring 5 neighbors Saturday.”It commits to delivering.
    “This team is going to be the top canvass team in the city.”It commits to a future possibility.

    Section 8· 5 min

    Put it all together: your commitment

    You’ve learned the skills. Now make a declaration, a promise and a plan.

    People

    Know your story of self. Listen 70% of the time. Make direct yes-or-no asks. Climb the ladder one rung at a time.

    Power

    Practice power-with. Delegate responsibility, not just tasks. Build teams with goals, roles and norms. Challenge and coach, don’t persecute or rescue.

    Purpose

    Put big rocks first. Cut issues you can win. Strategy before tactics. Connect each win to the long game. Keep assessments grounded.

    My volunteer commitment

    Share your commitment with your volunteer coordinator so they can match you to the right shifts and support your growth.
