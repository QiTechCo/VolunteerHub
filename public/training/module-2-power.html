Power: Stepping Into Leadership Together

What is power, how do we each stand more fully in it, and how do we build teams and campaigns that share it? This module covers power-with leadership, personal growth, strong teams, healthy conflict and how decisions really get made.

    Section 1· 10 min

    Power-over, power-under, power-with

    Key takeaways

    • “Power is the ability to achieve purpose.” (Dr. King)
    • Power-over = bossing and blaming · power-under = feeling powerless · power-with = side by side.
    • Power grows when it’s shared: “When you win, I win.”
    📖 Prefer to read? Open the full lesson

    Many of us feel uneasy about power because we’ve seen it used by some people at others’ expense. But at its core, power is simply the ability to get things done.

    “Power is the ability to achieve purpose.”— Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.

    Put another way, power is the ability to mobilize resources to meet needs. We need to build it to win. The training describes two forms:

    Dominant power

    Puts some people’s needs ahead of others’. It aims for compliance through positional and cultural power, and produces two patterns: power-over (domination) and power-under (victimhood). It often shows up as low morale, high turnover and us-vs-them thinking.

    Liberatory power

    Puts everyone’s needs first. It aims to unleash potential through power-within and power-with. It trades the illusion of control for truth-telling, transparency and support, which makes teams adaptive and resilient.

    Full comparison table
    Power-over (domination)Power-under (victimhood)Power-with (liberation)
    One up; few feel powerfulOne down; many feel powerlessSide by side; many feel powerful
    Power is finitePower is finitePower is infinite

    Objectification; certaintyApathy; “have to”Mutuality; “choose to”
    Harm, intimidation; hardeningHypervigilance; numbingHealing, supporting; vulnerability

    Sources cited in the packet: Steven Wineman (power-under/over/with), Cyndi Suarez (liberatory vs. dominant), Allison Porter and the IGNITE program.

    Finite vs. infinite power

    Finite mindset: “If you win, I lose.” People compete for power, hoard information, prize conformity and loyalty, and measure success only by winning. The result is deception, silos and low creativity.
    Infinite mindset: “When you win, I win.” Power grows when it’s shared through collaboration and communication. Differences are chances to learn, transparency is valued, and the team’s well-being counts as success.

    Language of finite and infinite power from Michael Broom, The Infinite Organization.

    “The most common way people give up their power is by thinking they don’t have any.”— Alice Walker

    Any leader has some power and many limits. Two unhealthy reactions are to boss people around with positional power, or to claim you have none. The better path is to own the power you have and use it to serve the mission and the people.

    Section 2· 7 min

    Conditioned tendencies and the bridge of uncertainty

    Key takeaways

    • Under pressure we default to fight, flight, freeze or fawn. Each can help or hurt.
    • Notice your default instead of reacting on autopilot.
    • Growing as a leader means crossing the bridge of uncertainty, again and again.
    📖 Prefer to read? Open the full lesson

    We all develop default ways of protecting ourselves. They helped us once, but they may not fit who we want to be now.

    Over our lives we form conditioned tendencies (default practices) in response to hardship, family, culture, loss or threats. We develop a practiced response to anger, to sadness, to power, to intimacy. These habits helped us survive and belong, but they were often formed when we had few ways to cope, so they may not fit our values today. They can make us harder to trust, less effective or narrower in how we lead.

    The good news: we can learn to notice our defaults instead of reacting automatically, and choose new practices that fit our values.

    Crossing doesn’t mean hard things stop happening. We may feel them more as we let go of old ways of numbing or avoiding. But we also gain more joy, confidence, belonging and dignity. Two tools help us cross: leadership declarations (for ourselves) and agitation (for others).

    🧩 Try it

    They grow from four instinctive threat responses. Most of us have one or two defaults. Flip each card:

    Fight

    tap to flip
    Healthy: assertiveness and clear boundaries.
    Reflexive: moving toward conflict with anger when it doesn’t help.

    Flight

    tap to flip
    Healthy: stepping away from real danger or a pointless fight.
    Reflexive: avoiding every hard conversation.

    Freeze

    tap to flip
    Healthy: slowing down to size up a situation carefully.
    Reflexive: feeling paralyzed, foggy or cut off from what’s happening.

    Fawn

    tap to flip
    Healthy: easing tension and connecting.
    Reflexive: people-pleasing and giving up your own needs to keep the peace.

    Section 3· 10 min

    Leadership declarations

    Key takeaways

    • Start with “I am a commitment to…”
    • Name the habits holding you back and the new practices you’ll repeat (about 300 reps to build muscle memory).
    • Set conditions of satisfaction with a date, so you know when you’ve arrived.
    📖 Prefer to read? Open the full lesson

    A declaration is a clear marker of where you’re trying to go. It keeps you on course when the path gets rocky.

    1

    Commitment statement

    A short, powerful statement of the future you want: “I am a commitment to…” It should be a little out of reach, involve some risk, and lead to things you deeply want.

    2

    Inhibiting practices

    The beliefs, reactions and habits keeping you from it. What do you need to release, stop, heal or give less energy to?

    3

    Enabling practices

    New habits you’ll build. They must be frequent and consistent: about 300 reps for muscle memory, 3,000 to make it part of who you are.

    4

    Conditions of satisfaction

    Measures with a deadline. What will you be able to do, and how often? What will others notice? How will you feel?

    Read the sample declaration from the training

    “I am a commitment to embracing uncertainty, openness and imperfection. I long to replace perfection with connection… so that I can be more present with myself and others, even amidst challenges.”

    Inhibiting beliefs: “I need to be perfect to be worthy; isolation is safer than outreach; planning for everything will protect me.” Practice: 15 minutes of mindfulness at least 5 days a week, plus box breathing or 5-4-3-2-1 grounding when old patterns show up.

    Enabling practices: take risks, practice vulnerability, ask for help. At least every two weeks, reach out for thought partnership when stuck, and try one thing outside your comfort zone at every training.

    Conditions of satisfaction: by a set date, laugh at mistakes at least 50% of the time; others see you as more relaxed and confident; you sleep better and stay present in situations that used to make you retreat.

    Section 4· 8 min

    Agitation: a caring challenge

    Key takeaways

    • Agitation is a caring challenge, rooted in their self-interest and a trusting relationship.
    • Four moves: Here’s where I see you → where you could be → do you agree? → what will you do?
    • Always have a 1:1 first. Without trust, it’s just irritation.
    📖 Prefer to read? Open the full lesson

    Agitation is a strategic conversation, grounded in mutual self-interest, that helps someone see the gap between who they want to be and how they’re acting, and gives them a choice about what to do next.

    A declaration is how we assess ourselves. Agitation is how we offer someone else our view of their leadership potential and what’s holding them back. It takes courage to tell someone a truth they may not want to hear, and it’s an act of care: we agitate people who matter to us.

    Irritation

    You’re just annoyed by how someone’s behavior affects you.

    Exploitation

    You’re pushing someone to do something that isn’t in their interest.

    Agitation

    Rooted in their self-interest and your investment in their growth, within a trusting relationship.

    The four moves

    1. “This is where I see you.”
    2. “This is where I think you could be, and should be.”
    3. “Is this right? Do you agree?”
    4. “Then what are you going to do about it?”
    The detailed version
    1. Share your own interest in their leadership.
    2. Share what you understand their self-interest to be, and check it. Are you right?
    3. Share your vision of where they could be as a leader. Do they agree?
    4. Ask what they think is keeping them from that potential.
    5. Share your observations of what’s in the way. Do they agree? If not, what else could it be?
    6. If you agree on the barrier, ask why they think it exists. Offer your view if it differs, and check it with them.
    7. If they agree, help them choose a sensible next step and practices to build new habits.

    Example: A volunteer knocks 60 doors every weekend but always says no when asked to lead a team. “You’re one of our most reliable people, and voters trust you. I think you could lead a canvass team, and it would help us reach twice as many neighbors. What’s stopping you?” … “It sounds like you worry about not having all the answers. None of us do. Would you co-lead with me next Saturday to try it out?”

    Section 5· 12 min

    Building powerful teams

    Key takeaways

    • Build snowflake teams: everyone owns a clear role and leads their own small team.
    • Delegate responsibility, not tasks. “Get 10 people there” beats “make 50 calls.”
    • Strong teams have shared goals, roles and norms.
    • Teams go through forming → storming → norming → performing → adjourning.
    📖 Prefer to read? Open the full lesson

    A neighborhood canvass hub might have a core team (a turf lead, a data lead and a volunteer-recruitment lead), and each of them leads their own small team. If one person steps away, the work keeps going.

    Identify, recruit, develop

    Identify

    Look for people with followers: who brings others along? Who do people tell you to “look for”? Organic leaders show up coaching teams, running church ministries, organizing block parties.

    Recruit

    Since followers make leaders, you can’t appoint one. Create chances for people to earn leadership, like “Can you bring 5 people to Thursday’s meeting?”

    Develop

    Structure the work so many people can learn to lead. Delegate responsibility, not just tasks.

    Task: “Would you make these 50 phone calls about the meeting?” The volunteer turns into a yo-yo: do this, come back, do that.
    Responsibility: “Would you take responsibility for getting 10 people to the meeting? Here’s a contact list, flyers and sign-up sheets.” They take it and run, and you support them.

    Risk small failures early to avoid big failures later. Learn who comes through at a 10-person meeting, not at the 5,000-person rally where only 50 show up. Clear goals, regular reports and debriefs turn every result into a lesson: “So, Mary, why did that work so well?”

    What motivates volunteer leaders

    Purpose

    Task significance: the work matters.
    Task identity: they own a whole piece with a visible result, like cooking the whole meal instead of just buying ingredients.
    Skill variety: they use and grow different skills.

    Responsibility

    Real freedom to plan their own work. Delegate outcomes, not tasks.

    Feedback

    Clear, specific, measurable information about results. Offer support without taking the responsibility back.

    Three qualities of effective teams

    Goals

    A clear, shared, meaningful purpose that every member can explain.

    Roles

    Each member owns a distinct piece that the whole depends on. No secret silos. A diverse team brings more.

    Norms

    Explicit agreements: how often we meet, where documents live, how we decide, and how we hold each other accountable.

    The five stages of team development

    Bruce Tuckman observed that teams move through these stages, and can slip back when things change, like when a new member joins.

    What you see

    Politeness, tentative joining, avoiding controversy, cliques. Excitement mixed with anxiety: “What’s expected of me? Why are we here?”

    What the team needs

    Mission and vision, specific objectives, roles, ground rules, expectations.

    Leadership required

    Provide structure and direction, give time to get acquainted, build confidence. The leader may still need to make decisions.

    To move on, members have to leave the comfort of safe topics and risk some conflict.

    What you see

    Arguing, jockeying for leadership, unclear roles, power struggles, unrealistic goals, defensiveness, “we’re not getting anywhere.”

    What the team needs

    Better listening, giving and receiving feedback, conflict resolution, a clearer purpose, a reset of roles and ground rules.

    Leadership required

    Name the conflict, teach conflict resolution, get members to take on more responsibility, offer support and praise. Shared leadership starts to emerge.

    To move on, members shift from “testing and proving” to problem-solving. The key skill is listening to teammates.

    What you see

    Agreed-upon processes, comfortable relationships, constructive criticism, real attempts at consensus, routines, milestones reached.

    What the team needs

    A decision-making process, shared problem-solving, everyone’s ideas and resources.

    Leadership required

    Shared leadership. Give feedback, allow less structure, ask everyone to contribute, keep building relationships.

    Creativity is high. The risk is that members start to fear the team breaking up and resist change.

    What you see

    Fully functional, self-organizing, flexible. Members know each other’s strengths, high commitment, fun and creativity.

    What the team needs

    Keep momentum, stay flexible, measure performance, share information, keep dialogue going.

    Leadership required

    Little direction needed. Members support each other and share new information.

    Not every team gets here. Those that do can work alone, in subgroups or all together.

    What you see

    Grief, slowing momentum, restlessness, bursts of energy, relief. For a campaign, this often comes after Election Day.

    What the team needs

    Evaluate the work, tie up loose ends, recognize and celebrate everyone’s contributions.

    Leadership required

    Listen well, reflect, plan for closure, and carry lessons into the next opportunity.

    An organizer’s job isn’t mainly to do things. It’s to help other people build the ability to do things together.

    Click each model to compare them:

    One person answers every question, makes every decision and does most of the work. It leads to burnout, wastes everyone else’s talent, keeps communication siloed, and collapses if that person leaves. Everyone ends up frustrated.

    A reaction to the single-leader model. Everyone acts, but with no clear structure for communication, decisions or who does what, people duplicate work, pull in different directions and reverse each other’s decisions.

    An interdependent leadership team. Members agree on their purpose, how they decide and how they govern themselves. Everyone has a clear role, which builds leaders and capacity. It scales: each core member leads their own team, with clear communication across the whole structure.

    Your new phone bank team argues about scripts and who’s in charge, and two people say “we’re not getting anywhere.” What stage is this, and what helps most?

    Section 6· 8 min

    Hierarchy and decision-making

    Key takeaways

    • Structure isn’t bad. Hidden structure is.
    • Healthy hierarchy needs visibility, feedback, responsibility and failsafes.
    • Decisions range from “decide and announce” to “delegate with constraints.” If you’re not sure who decides, ask.
    📖 Prefer to read? Open the full lesson

    Power-with doesn’t mean “no structure.” A hierarchy is just a clear way of organizing complex work.

    Every organization has structure. In “The Tyranny of Structurelessness,” Jo Freeman argues that “structureless” groups still have hidden hierarchies, and they’re usually less accountable and fair because only insiders know the rules.

    Four conditions for a healthy hierarchy

    Visibility

    Say plainly who’s responsible for what. If you’re unsure of the structure, ask.

    Feedback

    Feedback flows in all directions. It matters even more that people in authority receive it.

    Responsibility

    Decision-making authority follows responsibility and expertise.

    Failsafes

    Clear, fair ways to handle it when something goes wrong.

    Campaigns mix these. Message, policy positions and legal and compliance matters are decided by the candidate and staff. A team captain might hold a delegated decision within constraints, like choosing canvass times and pairings for their turf within the weekly door goal. When you’re not sure who decides, ask your coordinator. That’s visibility in action.

    Section 7· 6 min

    From the drama triangle to the empowerment triangle

    Key takeaways

    • In conflict we slip into persecutor, victim or rescuer roles.
    • Swap them for challenger, creator and coach.
    • A coach believes others can solve their own problems.

    🧩 Try it

    In conflict we often slip into one of three roles. Each has a healthier alternative. Flip each card to see it.

    Persecutor → Challenger

    tap to flip
    Persecutor: critical, blames others, steps in to dominate. Seen as a controlling bully.

    Challenger: still gives critical feedback, but to help others grow. States boundaries and expectations clearly, and swaps blame for curiosity and compassion.

    Victim → Creator

    tap to flip
    Victim: feels powerless and hopeless, complains without acting.

    Creator: focuses on outcomes instead of problems, owns their response, and takes steady steps to close the gap between today and the goal.

    Rescuer → Coach

    tap to flip
    Rescuer: “Let me help!” Saves victims in ways that keep them helpless, and can turn into a persecutor or victim when frustrated.

    Coach: sees the other person as capable, and asks questions that help them find their own solutions and focus on what they do want.

    Karpman’s Drama Triangle; the Empowerment Triangle is presented in the packet as the positive alternative.

    Which role is each volunteer playing?

    “Don’t worry, I’ll just redo your whole turf myself.”Taking over keeps the other person dependent. That’s the rescuer.
    “What do you think made that street hard? What would you try differently next time?”Questions that build the other person’s ability come from a coach.
    “Nobody ever tells me anything. What’s the point?”Powerless complaining is the victim role.
    “We agreed on 6 p.m. When you’re late, the team waits. Can we commit to on-time starts?”A clear boundary with a growth focus is the challenger.
    “You clearly don’t care. You’re the reason we’re behind.”Blame and attack are the persecutor role.
    “Turnout at our event was low. I’m going to try personal calls instead of texts next time.”Owning the result and taking the next step is the creator.

    Section 8· 8 min

    The three dimensions of power

    Key takeaways

    • 1st: winning the decision (elections, votes).
    • 2nd: setting the agenda (what even gets discussed).
    • 3rd: shaping “common sense” (the stories people believe). All three work together.
    📖 Prefer to read? Open the full lesson

    Power isn’t only who wins the vote. Political theorist Steven Lukes described three “faces” of power, and organizers use his framework to plan better.

    Direct political action. When interests clash, the side with more power gets the decision. This is the most visible dimension, and the one organizers know best. We build it by organizing people to press decision-makers and win: advocacy, public pressure and elections. Opponents build it too, through lobbying, spending and their own organizing.

    Winning the at-large seat, and then winning votes on the budget or housing bonds, is first-dimension power.

    Setting the agenda. Who decides what’s even up for debate? Sometimes it’s visible, like a proposal that dies in committee. Sometimes it’s invisible, when ideas never enter public discussion at all. Changing the agenda takes strong organizations that can keep an agenda in front of people over time, and coalitions of many groups aligned around shared goals.

    Which issues come up at council meetings and candidate forums? Dimple’s priorities help set the agenda for the city’s debate.

    Shaping worldview. Everyday decisions rest on “common sense”: mental maps built from worldviews, ideologies (beliefs about how society should work) and narratives (the big-picture stories we tell). Common sense isn’t fixed. People create and re-create it, so it can change. The third dimension shapes what people want to put on the table in the first place.

    “Your safety shouldn’t depend on your zip code” is a narrative. Every conversation you have helps shape how neighbors think about what Charlotte can be.

    The dimensions are nested (the 3rd shapes the 2nd, which shapes the 1st) and they feed each other: big first-dimension wins can shift public debate and common sense. So power-building isn’t step-by-step. Like length, width and depth, strong campaigns work on all three at once: winning decisions, shaping the agenda and changing the story.

    The packet adds its own examples from the progressive movement (for example healthcare framed as a right, and debates over policing). We use local campaign examples instead.

    Section 9· 8 min

    Winning is the first step: arenas of decision-making

    Key takeaways

    • Decisions happen in six arenas: electoral, legislative, administrative, judicial, communications, economic.
    • Your work now is in the electoral arena.
    • Winning is the first step. Wins last only if someone watches how they’re carried out.
    📖 Prefer to read? Open the full lesson

    Election Day isn’t the finish line. A win on paper can be undone if nobody pays attention to how it’s carried out.

    The training defines governing power as the ability to win and keep influence across several arenas where decisions are made, so you can deliver lasting results. It builds on Changing States by Manuel Pastor, Jennifer Ito and Madeline Wander, which names six arenas. Click each one:

    Key lesson: Many groups are ready to win a policy, a lawsuit or an election, and then move straight on to the next fight. But “the real battle is just beginning.” A legislative win can be undone in implementation, and a campaign win can be reversed by a court. Keeping what we win is what governing power is about.

    The packet frames governing power around replacing “neoliberalism” with a new progressive governing paradigm. We’ve kept the practical framework and left out the ideological framing.

    🧩 Try it

    Electoral arena

    What it is: where voters choose who holds office or vote directly on measures.
    Building power: educate and turn out voters for candidates who share our values; recruit candidates from the community.
    Governing: a reliable majority of voters who decide key races.

    This is where you work now: every door, call and ride to the polls.

    Legislative arena

    What it is: where elected officials make or change laws.
    Building power: work with legislators to pass policies and build coalitions of elected allies.
    Governing: champions leading the governing majority.

    For Charlotte, that’s City Council votes on budgets, housing bonds and ordinances.

    Administrative arena

    What it is: where laws become rules and programs are carried out.
    Building power: learn the rules and bureaucracy so community voices are heard in how policies are carried out.
    Governing: trusted, skilled leaders appointed to implement and enforce.

    After a vote, city departments run the programs. Initiatives Dimple has backed, like SAFE Charlotte and HIRE Charlotte, succeed or fail in how they’re carried out.

    Judicial & constitutional arena

    What it is: where laws are interpreted and applied.
    Building power: strategic litigation, watching the courts, supporting good judges.
    Governing: enough fair-minded judges to shape how laws are interpreted.

    Communications arena

    What it is: where competing groups use stories to shape values and “common sense.”
    Building power: expose misleading narratives and lift up stories that bring people together around solutions.
    Governing: our values become the widely shared common sense.

    Economic arena

    What it is: where wages, working life and consumer choices are decided.
    Building power: workers organizing and bargaining together, and policies that expand opportunity and accountability.
    Governing: working people have real influence over the standards that shape their lives.
